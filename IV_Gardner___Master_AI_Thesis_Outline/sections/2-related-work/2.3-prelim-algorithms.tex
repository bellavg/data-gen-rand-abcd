\subsection{Synthesis Algorithms}
\label{sec:prelim:algorithms}

\subsubsection{Rewriting, Refactoring, Resubstitution, and Balancing}
\label{sec:prelim:algorithms:primitives}
Synthesis scripts are assembled from four local, function-preserving transformations
\citep{mishchenko2006rewriting}. A cut of gate $v$ is a node set through which every PI-to-$v$
path passes, and it is $K$-feasible when it has at most $K$ members, so a cut computes a
Boolean function of at most $K$ variables and a wider cut reasons over a larger window.

Rewriting replaces the logic inside a $K$-feasible cut with the smallest precomputed
equivalent, accepting the replacement only on a net gain
\citep{bjesse2004compression,mishchenko2006rewriting}. Refactoring makes the same exchange
with the MFFC of~\eqref{eq:prelim:mffc} as the unit, which is larger than a cut and therefore
restructures more of the network at once. Resubstitution re-expresses a gate through gates
already present in the network, its divisors, and removes the MFFC left redundant when a valid
re-expression is found \citep{mishchenko2006scalable}. Balancing restructures AND trees under
associativity to reduce the depth $\ell(G)$ of~\eqref{eq:prelim:level} at an unchanged
function.

The four differ in the unit they act on and in the objective they serve. Rewriting,
refactoring and resubstitution reduce node count; balancing reduces depth, and can raise node
count while doing so. None changes the Boolean function, so any sequence of them is sound, and
their gains do not commute: each acts on the structure its predecessors produced.

Structural hashing carries a signature on every AND gate \citep{kuehlmann2002robust},
\begin{equation}
    \kappa(v) = \bigl\{\, (u, \mathrm{inv}(u, v)) : u \in \operatorname{fi}(v) \,\bigr\},
    \label{eq:prelim:strash}
\end{equation}
and maintains $\kappa(u) \neq \kappa(v)$ for distinct AND gates on construction. Two gates
over the same fanins with the same inversions are therefore never both present: one-hop
structural redundancy is eliminated as the network is built, not by a later pass.

Functional equivalence is the stronger relation,
\begin{equation}
    u \equiv v \iff b_u = b_v \text{ under every PI assignment},
    \label{eq:prelim:funceq}
\end{equation}
with $b_u$ as in~\eqref{eq:prelim:inversion}. A Functionally Reduced AIG (FRAIG) merges every
such pair \citep{mishchenko2005fraig}, using simulation to refute candidates cheaply and
satisfiability solving to prove the survivors. Two nodes are functionally complementary when
$b_u = \neg b_v$ throughout, and a FRAIG merges those pairs too.

The distinction between the two relations matters because it separates what is cheap from what
is expensive. Structural hashing decides~\eqref{eq:prelim:funceq} only in the special case
$\kappa(u) = \kappa(v)$, which local inspection settles in constant time. The general case is a
semantic property that no local inspection reveals, and establishing it is as hard as the
equivalence checking synthesis tools invoke a solver for. Any procedure that merges nodes must
therefore declare which of the two lines it works on, because the structural line is free and
the functional line performs optimization.

\subsubsection{Optimization Objectives: Area, Power, Delay}
\label{sec:prelim:synthesis:objectives}
Area, power and delay are the costs synthesis is judged on and, before technology mapping,
none is physical: each is tracked through a structural proxy, node count $|\mathcal{V}(G)|$
for area and depth $\ell(G)$ of~\eqref{eq:prelim:level} for delay. The ideal is the smallest
network computing the same function,
\begin{equation}
    G^{\star} \in \arg\min_{H \,\equiv\, G} \bigl| \mathcal{V}(H) \bigr|,
    \label{eq:prelim:objective}
\end{equation}
which no practical script attains: a script instead returns some $S(G) \equiv G$ with
$|\mathcal{V}(S(G))| \leq |\mathcal{V}(G)|$. Both proxies are inherited downstream, technology
mapping binding fewer cells from a smaller network and a shorter critical path from a
shallower one.

\subsubsection{Synthesis Orchestration}
\label{sec:prelim:algorithms:orchestrate}
A conventional script fixes one ordering of whole-graph passes in advance: balancing runs over
the entire network, then rewriting, then refactoring, and every gate is subject to the same
sequence in the same order. Synthesis orchestration moves that choice into the graph
\citep{li2024orchestration}. Instead of applying one transformation to the whole network at a
time, it evaluates rewriting, refactoring and resubstitution \emph{at each AND gate}, commits
whichever scores best there, and repeats the sweep for a bounded number of passes. The
granularity is the distinguishing feature: the decision is per node rather than per pass.

The reason this pays is that the three transformations suit different local structure.
Rewriting suits a small cut for which a better precomputed equivalent exists; refactoring suits
a large fanout-free region worth re-expressing whole; resubstitution suits a gate whose
function is already computed among its divisors. A whole-graph pass applies one of them
everywhere, including at gates where another would have paid more, and the orderings that
matter are therefore decided globally when the opportunity is local.

Two selection criteria are defined for choosing among the three at a gate, a priority ordering
over the transformations and a local-greedy choice by immediate cost. Balancing is not among
the orchestrated transformations: depth reduction remains a separate whole-graph pass, so
orchestration reduces node count and does not target depth.

\subsubsection{Fixed and Randomized Script Families}
\label{sec:prelim:algorithms:others}
Beyond orchestration, the scripts distributed with a synthesis system \citep{brayton2010abc}
fall into two families, and the distinction governs whether a script's output is a function of
its input alone.

A \emph{fixed} script is a deterministic sequence of the transformations
of~\ref{sec:prelim:algorithms:primitives}, written once and applied unchanged. Its behaviour is
fully determined by the ordering its author chose. The escalating-window family is
representative: balancing alternates with resubstitution whose cut bound $K$ widens across the
sequence, from $K = 6$ to $K = 12$, so early steps make cheap local exchanges and later steps
reason over larger windows on the structure those exchanges produced
\citep{mishchenko2006scalable}. Such scripts also admit \emph{zero-cost} variants of each
transformation, which accept a replacement leaving node count unchanged rather than requiring a
strict gain, so the search can cross a plateau instead of halting on it, and they can be
constrained to preserve depth while doing so.

A \emph{randomized} script searches instead of following a fixed recipe. Restructuring passes
are applied under a wall-clock budget with randomized choices, and the best network encountered
is returned. Two consequences follow, and both matter for anyone reading a corpus built with
such a script: the output is a sample rather than a function of the input, so the same circuit
optimized twice need not give the same result; and reproducing a particular output requires the
random seed, which is not part of the script.
