\subsection{And-Inverter Graphs}
\label{sec:prelim:aig}

\subsubsection{Definition and Structure}
\label{sec:prelim:aig:definition}
An AIG is a DAG $G = (\mathcal{V}, \mathcal{E})$ representing a Boolean network over the
functionally complete basis $\{\wedge, \neg\}$ \citep{mishchenko2006rewriting}: every internal
node is a two-input AND gate, and every edge carries an optional inversion. Conjunction is
therefore the only operation that costs a node, and negation costs none. Completeness does
not buy uniqueness: one Boolean function admits many AIGs, and which one an optimizer is
handed bounds what it can remove, a sensitivity known as structural bias
\citep{gardner2025bias}. How far a given network can be reduced is thus a property of the
graph rather than of the function it computes.

The fanin and fanout of a node are
\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{fi}(v) = \{\, u \in \mathcal{V} : (u, v) \in \mathcal{E} \,\},
    \qquad
    \operatorname{fo}(v) = \{\, w \in \mathcal{V} : (v, w) \in \mathcal{E} \,\},
    \label{eq:prelim:fanin}
\end{equation}
with $|\operatorname{fi}(v)| = 2$ at every AND gate, $\operatorname{fi}(v) = \emptyset$ at
every \emph{Primary Input} (PI) and at the constant, and $|\operatorname{fi}(v)| = 1$ at every
\emph{Primary Output} (PO). The constant is the single source node supplying the logical
values $0$ and $1$; the PIs are the free variables of the network; an AND gate computes the
conjunction of the two values delivered on its fanin edges; the POs are the signals the
network exposes to whatever consumes it. Together the PIs and POs form the \emph{boundary} of
the circuit, the only signals visible from outside it.

Acyclicity yields a topological order $\prec$, with $u \prec v$ for every
$(u, v) \in \mathcal{E}$, so signals flow from the PIs to the POs and never return. POs are
materialised as nodes here rather than as pointers into the AND network, as is also common.
That choice is why the node role of~\eqref{eq:prelim:role} takes four values instead of
three, and it places the boundary inside $|\mathcal{V}|$.

\subsubsection{Node Types and Inverted Edges}
\label{sec:prelim:aig:nodes}
Two labellings are carried on every graph. A node role
\begin{equation}
    \tau \colon \mathcal{V} \rightarrow
    \{\, \mathrm{const},\ \mathrm{PI},\ \mathrm{AND},\ \mathrm{PO} \,\}
    \label{eq:prelim:role}
\end{equation}
separates the constant, the PIs, the AND gates and the POs, and an inversion flag
$\mathrm{inv} \colon \mathcal{E} \rightarrow \{0, 1\}$ marks each edge as regular or inverted.
Inversion is semantic: writing $b_u \in \{0,1\}$ for the value produced at $u$, edge
$e = (u, v)$ delivers to $v$ the value
\begin{equation}
    b_u \oplus \mathrm{inv}(e),
    \label{eq:prelim:inversion}
\end{equation}
so two edges agreeing in their endpoints but differing in $\mathrm{inv}$ carry complementary
signals. Inversion is therefore semantic rather than decorative: it is part of the function
the graph denotes, not an annotation on it. A small AIG carrying both labellings is drawn
in~\ref{fig:aig_example}.

\subsubsection{Structural Properties: Levels, Logic Cones, Fanout}
\label{sec:prelim:aig:properties}
The topological level of a node is its longest distance from the input frontier,
\begin{equation}
    \ell(v) =
    \begin{cases}
        0 & \text{if } \operatorname{fi}(v) = \emptyset, \\[2pt]
        1 + \max_{u \in \operatorname{fi}(v)} \ell(u) & \text{otherwise,}
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:prelim:level}
\end{equation}
and the depth of the graph is $\ell(G) = \max_{v \in \mathcal{V}} \ell(v)$. The drawn height
of a gate in~\ref{fig:aig_example} is exactly $\ell$. Level is an absolute coordinate: every
node has a well defined distance from the input frontier, which a general graph does not
supply, and it orders the network by how much logic must resolve before a gate can be
evaluated.

Writing $u \rightsquigarrow v$ for a directed path, of length zero when $u = v$, the fanin
cone (the logic or causal cone) and fanout cone of $v$ are
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{C}(v) = \{\, u \in \mathcal{V} : u \rightsquigarrow v \,\},
    \qquad
    \mathcal{F}(v) = \{\, w \in \mathcal{V} : v \rightsquigarrow w \,\}.
    \label{eq:prelim:cone}
\end{equation}
Both cones contain $v$ itself. The fanin cone is the set of gates on which $v$ functionally
depends, and~\eqref{eq:prelim:level} measures the longest path through it. Cones are nested
along paths: deleting a single edge on a long path removes gates from the fanin cone of
everything downstream of it, whether or not the result resembles the original under an
undirected measure of similarity.

Fanout degree $|\operatorname{fo}(v)|$ separates gates whose output is consumed once from
gates whose output is shared. Chains of single-fanout gates carry no sharing information.
Multi-fanout gates are the points at which logic is reused, and they are what constrains local
restructuring: a gate feeding several consumers cannot be rewritten for the benefit of one
without affecting the others, so shared logic is expensive to change and cheap to keep.

Reconvergence is the same property seen from below. A gate $v$ is a reconvergence point of $u$
when two internally disjoint directed paths run from $u$ to $v$, so a single signal reaches
$v$ along more than one route. AIGs are dense in reconvergence, because structural hashing
(\ref{sec:prelim:algorithms:primitives}) shares every duplicated subexpression instead of
replicating it \citep{kuehlmann2002robust}. Reconvergence is what makes an AIG resist being
treated as a tree: the fanin cones of two gates overlap rather than partitioning the network,
so any procedure that groups gates must decide what to do with logic that several groups have
a claim on.

Where a fanout cone reconverges is a question of post-dominance
\citep{lengauer1979dominators}. Appending a virtual sink $t$ after every PO and extending
$\prec$ to it, the immediate post-dominator of $v$ is
\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{ipdom}(v) = \min_{\prec}
        \{\, u \neq v : \text{every } v \rightsquigarrow t \text{ path meets } u \,\},
    \label{eq:prelim:ipdom}
\end{equation}
the gate at which $\mathcal{F}(v)$ reconverges, or $t$ when it never does. Post-dominance
gives a canonical answer to the question reconvergence raises: every gate has exactly one
immediate post-dominator, so the network admits a well defined partition into regions that
reconverge at a common point, and a procedure grouping gates can use that partition instead of
choosing arbitrarily.

The Maximal Fanout-Free Cone (MFFC) of a root gate $v$ collects the gates feeding $v$ and
nothing else \citep{mishchenko2006rewriting},
\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{MFFC}(v) = \{\, u \in \mathcal{C}(v) :
        \text{every path } u \rightsquigarrow t \text{ passes through } v \,\},
    \label{eq:prelim:mffc}
\end{equation}
so removing $v$ from the network removes exactly $\operatorname{MFFC}(v)$ and nothing more.
That property makes the MFFC the natural unit of local restructuring: it is the largest region
whose fate is decided entirely by one gate, and therefore the largest region that can be
replaced without consulting the rest of the network
(\ref{sec:prelim:algorithms:primitives}).

\subsubsection{Structural Distinctions from General Graph Classes}
\label{sec:prelim:aig:distinct}
Five properties separate an AIG from the citation, social and knowledge graphs on which the
graph reduction literature was developed. Each bears on what a method designed for those
classes can assume.

(i) An AIG is directed and acyclic. Machinery built for undirected graphs does not transfer
unchanged, because a quotient of a DAG need not itself be acyclic, and a reduction computed on
the undirected projection discards the orientation that carries the logic.

(ii) Depth, not width, is the binding dimension. The critical path is a longest directed path
from a PI to a PO, and its length is the depth $\ell(G)$ of~\eqref{eq:prelim:level}: the
quantity balancing exists to shorten (\ref{sec:prelim:algorithms:primitives}). After $L$
message-passing rounds a node representation depends on its $L$-hop neighbourhood and on
nothing else (\ref{sec:prelim:gnn:mp}), so a gate reads the whole of the logic it depends on,
$\mathcal{C}_L(v) = \mathcal{C}(v)$, only when
\begin{equation}
    L \ge \ell(v).
    \label{eq:prelim:reach}
\end{equation}
Logic circuits routinely reach depths in the hundreds or thousands, whereas the round counts at
which message passing remains trainable are in the single digits, so~\eqref{eq:prelim:reach}
fails for all but the shallowest gates. Graph classes on which reduction was developed are
shallow and wide, and the inequality is not binding on them.

(iii) Edges carry function. The inversion of~\eqref{eq:prelim:inversion} is part of what the
graph represents, so a merge that averages or discards $\mathrm{inv}$ changes the Boolean
function rather than approximating it. Elsewhere an edge attribute is usually a weight or a
relation type that a reduction may smooth over; here it is a logical operator.

(iv) The boundary is fixed. The PIs and POs are the signals by which the circuit is connected
to the rest of a design, so they are not free to be renamed, merged or removed: doing so
changes which function the network computes for its surroundings, rather than coarsening its
internal structure. General graphs distinguish no such privileged set of nodes.

(v) The graph carries no surplus density. Fanin cardinality is fixed by the basis, so
\begin{equation}
    |\mathcal{E}| = 2 \left| \tau^{-1}(\mathrm{AND}) \right|
        + \left| \tau^{-1}(\mathrm{PO}) \right| < 2 \left| \mathcal{V} \right|,
    \label{eq:prelim:edges}
\end{equation}
so the edge count is bounded by twice the node count and one message-passing round costs
$\Theta(|\mathcal{V}|)$ at fixed width. An AIG is therefore already as sparse as a graph of its
node count can be, and removing edges alone cannot take that cost below the same order at any
retention ratio (\ref{sec:prelim:reduction:sparsification}). The social and citation graphs
that motivated edge sparsification are dense by comparison, which is what gives edge removal
something to remove.
