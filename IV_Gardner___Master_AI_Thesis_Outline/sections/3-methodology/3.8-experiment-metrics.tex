\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:method:experiment:metrics}
Four groups of metrics are reported. Accuracy and ranking are always reported together: on
a coarsened EDA graph, CTS-Bench \citep{cts_bench2026} observed mean absolute error barely
moving while $R^2$ fell below zero, exactly the failure a reduction study risks missing if
it reports error alone.

\paragraph{Notation.}
Let $\mathcal{D} = \{G_i\}_{i=1}^{N}$ be an evaluation set, $y_i = y(G_i) \in [0, 1]$ the
optimizability target of~\ref{sec:prelim:formalization}, $f_\theta$ the trained encoder and
$R$ the reduction operator, with $R = \operatorname{id}$ for the unreduced baseline.
Residuals are written $r_i = \hat{y}_i - y_i$, and $\bar{y} = N^{-1} \sum_{i} y_i$ is the
target mean over $\mathcal{D}$.

\paragraph{Evaluation protocol.}
Each trained configuration is evaluated in two passes over the test set, differing only in
the input given the same weights:
\begin{align}
    \hat{y}_i^{\text{match}} &= f_\theta\!\left( R(G_i) \right),
    \label{eq:method:pass-matched} \\
    \hat{y}_i^{\text{full}} &= f_\theta\!\left( G_i \right).
    \label{eq:method:pass-full}
\end{align}
The matched pass~\eqref{eq:method:pass-matched} sees reduced graphs, the full-graph
pass~\eqref{eq:method:pass-full} unreduced ones, and their difference isolates the cost of
the structural shift from the cost of the reduction itself.

\paragraph{Which measurement answers which question.}
Accuracy and ranking on the matched pass answer RQ1 (unreduced) and RQ3 (reduced), recomputed
under each split protocol for RQ1a. Retention~\eqref{eq:prelim:retention}, offline cost, the
amortisation threshold~\eqref{eq:method:amortisation}, peak memory, step time and
throughput~\eqref{eq:method:throughput} answer RQ2. RQ4 is the paired
gap~\eqref{eq:method:paired-gap} between a domain-informed configuration and its generic
control, with the receptive field~\eqref{eq:method:receptive} as the mechanism test H1
requires. RQ5 is the cross-state drop-off~\eqref{eq:method:dropoff} between the two passes.

\subsubsection{Predictive Accuracy}
\label{sec:method:experiment:metrics:accuracy}
The training objective is the smooth L1 loss, transition point $\beta = 0.01$,
\begin{equation}
    \ell_\beta(r) = \begin{cases}
        r^{2} / (2\beta), & |r| < \beta, \\
        |r| - \beta / 2, & \text{otherwise},
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:method:smoothl1}
\end{equation}
reported on validation and test for comparability with the training curves. Since the
target occupies $[0, 1]$, all but the smallest residuals fall in the linear branch
of~\eqref{eq:method:smoothl1}: outside one percentage point the objective behaves as a mean
absolute error. Accuracy proper is reported as
\begin{align}
    \mathrm{MAE} &= \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \left| r_i \right|,
    \label{eq:method:mae} \\
    \mathrm{RMSE} &= \sqrt{\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} r_i^{2}},
    \label{eq:method:rmse} \\
    R^{2} &= 1 - \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N} r_i^{2}}
        {\sum_{i=1}^{N} \left( y_i - \bar{y} \right)^{2}}.
    \label{eq:method:r2}
\end{align}
MAE and RMSE are on the label's own scale, so $\mathrm{RMSE} = 0.05$ is five percentage
points of optimizability. By the power mean inequality $\mathrm{MAE} \leq \mathrm{RMSE}$, so
$\mathrm{RMSE} / \mathrm{MAE}$ measures how much a few large errors carry the average.
Equation~\eqref{eq:method:r2} divides the same squared error by the target's own spread:
$R^{2} = 0$ is the constant predictor $\hat{y}_i = \bar{y}$, and $R^{2} < 0$ is worse than
that. $R^{2}$ is computed for validation and test only, never per training batch, since a
batch's variance is not a meaningful reference for the target's spread.

\paragraph{Comparisons derived from these metrics.}
Let $M(c)$ be any of~\eqref{eq:method:mae}, \eqref{eq:method:rmse}, \eqref{eq:method:r2}
or~\eqref{eq:method:spearman} for configuration $c$, and $\operatorname{id}$ the full-graph
baseline. Every relative accuracy figure is one of
\begin{align}
    \varrho(c) &= \frac{M(c)}{M(\operatorname{id})},
    \label{eq:method:retained} \\
    \Delta_{\mathrm{state}}(c) &= M\big( \hat{y}^{\text{full}}(c) \big)
        - M\big( \hat{y}^{\text{match}}(c) \big),
    \label{eq:method:dropoff} \\
    \Delta_{\mathrm{pair}}(c_{\mathrm{d}}, c_{\mathrm{g}}) &= M(c_{\mathrm{d}})
        - M(c_{\mathrm{g}}),
    \label{eq:method:paired-gap}
\end{align}
the fraction of baseline retained, the cross-state drop-off between the passes
of~\eqref{eq:method:pass-matched} and~\eqref{eq:method:pass-full}, and the gap between a
domain-informed configuration $c_{\mathrm{d}}$ and its generic control $c_{\mathrm{g}}$
(also RQ1a's protocol inflation, taken between split protocols rather than reductions).
Retention uses a ratio, the other two a difference, since $R^{2}$ crosses zero, where a
ratio is meaningless.

\paragraph{Variance already carried by the corpus.}
A score is evidence of structural reading only beyond what the corpus gives away free. For
a partition of $\mathcal{D}$ into groups $g$, the group-mean predictor
$\hat{y}_i = \bar{y}_{g(i)}$ scores
\begin{equation}
    R^{2}_{\mathrm{grp}} = 1 - \frac{\sum_{i} \left( y_i - \bar{y}_{g(i)} \right)^{2}}
        {\sum_{i} \left( y_i - \bar{y} \right)^{2}},
    \label{eq:method:group-r2}
\end{equation}
the share of label variance explained by group membership alone. Reporting
\eqref{eq:method:r2} beside~\eqref{eq:method:group-r2} for the source script and tier
separates a score's circuit-attributable part from what a lookup table would reach.

\paragraph{Persisted predictions and stratified re-scoring.}
The evaluation sweep writes every per-graph prediction to disk, so every metric above can be
recomputed on any test subset from the same forward pass. This matters since the pooled
label is severely skewed (roughly half the test graphs at $y_i = 0$), so a pooled $R^2$ is
dominated by near-identical labels and can look respectable while saying nothing about the
graphs that matter in practice. Each accuracy claim is therefore also reported on three
subsets: designs the script can still change, graphs with $y_i > 0$, and graphs from a
single upstream script, applied uniformly to every configuration.

\subsubsection{Ranking Quality}
\label{sec:method:experiment:metrics:ranking}
Spearman rank correlation between predicted and true optimizability, the Pearson
correlation of the midrank vectors,
\begin{equation}
    \rho = \frac{\operatorname{cov}\!\left( \operatorname{rk}(\hat{y}),
        \operatorname{rk}(y) \right)}
        {\sigma_{\operatorname{rk}(\hat{y})} \, \sigma_{\operatorname{rk}(y)}}.
    \label{eq:method:spearman}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:method:spearman} is unchanged by any strictly increasing map applied to
$\hat{y}$, so it is reported alongside the error metrics, not in place of them: a model
whose predictions have collapsed toward $\bar{y}$, the failure mode a reduction study
should expect, holds $\rho$ while $\mathrm{RMSE}$ and $R^2$ degrade. Ranking is also closer
to use: a practitioner deciding where to spend synthesis effort needs circuit ordering, not
calibrated values.

The metric ranks \emph{circuits} under one fixed script, not \emph{scripts} for one
circuit, the script-selection task the motivation invokes and this thesis does not attempt
(\ref{sec:intro:scope:prediction}).

\subsubsection{Efficiency and Resource Metrics}
\label{sec:method:experiment:metrics:efficiency}
Three quantities are reported: peak device memory, per-graph step time $t(G_i)$, and
inference throughput
\begin{equation}
    \tau = \frac{\left| \mathcal{D} \right|}{T(\mathcal{D})},
    \label{eq:method:throughput}
\end{equation}
graphs scored per second over an evaluation pass of duration $T$. Epoch time is logged but
not compared, since the budget each run consumed varied and an epoch carries the training
schedule as much as the reduction. $t$ and $\tau$ are the timing quantities that compare,
and $t$ also enters~\eqref{eq:method:amortisation}.

Two instruments produce these numbers, and which one produced a figure fixes what it may be
compared against. Throughput and inference peak memory come from the evaluation sweep under
the fixed node budget of~\ref{sec:method:experiment:batching}, identical across
configurations. Step time and step memory instead come from a separate benchmark at one
graph per batch and no budget, since a node budget packs a different number of graphs per
batch for every configuration and would make per-batch aggregates incomparable. One graph
per batch matches each reduced graph to its unreduced counterpart by identity, as the
paired statistics of~\ref{sec:method:experiment:metrics:stats} require, at the price of a
controlled per-graph cost rather than an estimate of wall-clock training cost.

\paragraph{Memory quantities reported.}
Peak \emph{allocated} memory, the high-water mark of live tensors, is the closest measure of
actual demand. Peak \emph{reserved} memory, what the caching allocator has taken from the
driver, is never smaller and reflects allocation history as much as demand. Allocated is the
primary figure; reserved sets the out-of-memory boundary and is recorded alongside it. Both
are taken against a \emph{memory floor} $m_{\mathrm{floor}}$ (parameters, optimizer state
and device context, measured after warmup with no graph resident), so the marginal cost of
the graph itself is
\begin{equation}
    \Delta m = m_{\mathrm{peak}} - m_{\mathrm{floor}}.
    \label{eq:method:marginal-memory}
\end{equation}
$\Delta m$, not $m_{\mathrm{peak}}$, is the quantity a reduction can move, since
$m_{\mathrm{floor}}$ is fixed and identical across configurations, and savings quoted
against $m_{\mathrm{peak}}$ are diluted by that additive constant. The paired savings
of~\eqref{eq:method:paired-saving} are taken on $m_{\mathrm{peak}}$, so every reported
saving is a lower bound on~\eqref{eq:method:marginal-memory}.

\subsubsection{Reduction Quality Metrics}
\label{sec:method:experiment:metrics:reduction}
The node and edge retention ratios $\eta_{\mathcal{V}}$ and $\eta_{\mathcal{E}}$
of~\eqref{eq:prelim:retention} are reported separately, not collapsed into one compression
figure, together with the offline wall-clock cost of computing the reduction.

The offline cost matters independently of the training saving. Let $c_R$ be the cost of
computing and storing one graph's reduction, and $t_{\operatorname{id}}$, $t_R$ the
per-graph step cost on the unreduced and reduced graph. One epoch visits each graph once, so
over $n$ epochs reducing is cheaper than not reducing once
\begin{equation}
    n > n^{\star} = \frac{c_R}{t_{\operatorname{id}} - t_R}.
    \label{eq:method:amortisation}
\end{equation}
Reporting the amortisation threshold $n^{\star}$ rather than a raw offline cost states the
trade-off in the terms that decide it: \eqref{eq:method:amortisation} is undefined for
$t_R \geq t_{\operatorname{id}}$, so a reduction that does not shorten a step never
amortises, however cheap to compute. The reduced graph is stored once and reloaded, so $n$
accumulates over every epoch of every run that reuses it.

\paragraph{Effective receptive field.}
Hypothesis H1 claims coarsening helps by contracting paths, a claim needing its own
measurement. For a node $v$, let
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{C}_k(v) = \left\{ u \in \mathcal{V} : u \rightsquigarrow v
        \text{ along a directed path of length at most } k \right\}
    \label{eq:method:cone}
\end{equation}
be its $k$-hop fanin cone, and $m_u = \mathbf{1}^{\top} x_u$ the member count of node $u$
(from the count-based feature schema of~\ref{sec:method:architecture}, equal to $1$ on an
unreduced graph). The metric is the member-weighted mean cone size over a sampled node set
$\mathcal{S} \subset \mathcal{V}(G)$,
\begin{equation}
    \bar{c}_k(G) = \frac{1}{\left| \mathcal{S} \right|} \sum_{v \in \mathcal{S}}
        \; \sum_{u \in \mathcal{C}_k(v)} m_u,
    \label{eq:method:receptive}
\end{equation}
evaluated at $k = L$ encoder layers, before and after reduction. Weighting by $m_u$ rather
than counting super-nodes is what makes the comparison meaningful, since it counts original
gates in both states. H1 predicts $\bar{c}_L(R(G)) > \bar{c}_L(G)$ under summarization and
$\bar{c}_L(R(G)) \leq \bar{c}_L(G)$ under sparsification, and unlike a DAG longest-path
metric, \eqref{eq:method:receptive} is well defined for every method.

\subsubsection{Statistical Treatment}
\label{sec:method:experiment:metrics:stats}
Every reported figure is an estimate over a finite set of test circuits, given with a
$95\%$ percentile bootstrap interval. For a statistic $\hat{\theta}$ computed on
$\mathcal{D}$, with $\hat{\theta}^{*(1)}, \dots, \hat{\theta}^{*(B)}$ its values on
$B = 2000$ resamples drawn with replacement, the interval is the pair of empirical
quantiles
\begin{equation}
    \left[ \hat{\theta}^{*}_{(\alpha / 2)}, \;
        \hat{\theta}^{*}_{(1 - \alpha / 2)} \right],
    \qquad \alpha = 0.05,
    \label{eq:method:bootstrap}
\end{equation}
with $\alpha$ fixed at that value everywhere. The percentile form is used rather than a
normal approximation, since neither statistic it is applied to is close to normal:
\eqref{eq:method:r2} is a ratio of sums of squares over a label with a point mass at zero,
and the per-graph savings below are skewed across graph sizes spanning orders of magnitude.

Efficiency comparisons are additionally paired per graph against the full-graph baseline:
for each test graph, the saving in an efficiency quantity $q$ (peak memory or step time) is
taken relative to that graph's own baseline measurement,
\begin{equation}
    \delta_i = 100 \left( 1 - \frac{q_R(G_i)}{q_{\operatorname{id}}(G_i)} \right),
    \label{eq:method:paired-saving}
\end{equation}
and $\{\delta_i\}$ is summarised by its mean with~\eqref{eq:method:bootstrap} and a
two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test of $H_0 \colon \operatorname{med}(\delta) = 0$. The
pairing is by construction, since both configurations are measured on the same graph, and
the skew is why a rank test replaces a paired $t$-test \citep{demsar2006statistical}.

Accuracy is reported with the same interval, and what it covers must be said plainly: it is
narrower than it appears. Resampling $\mathcal{D}$ quantifies sampling error over circuits,
the error a single training run does allow to be estimated. It does not cover run-to-run
variance from initialisation, data order and nondeterministic kernels, since each
configuration here is trained exactly once (\ref{sec:method:experiment:reproducibility}).
Single-run comparisons of this kind have been shown to reverse under reseeding
\citep{reimers2017scoredistributions,bouthillier2021variance}, so a difference smaller than
this unmeasured training variance cannot be called real. RQ4 is where that bites hardest,
its expected effect being the smallest in the thesis: the defensible report is the paired
gap~\eqref{eq:method:paired-gap} with its sampling interval and an explicit statement that
seed variance is missing (\ref{sec:discussion:limitations:reproducibility}).

Intervals are the primary report, not $p$-values: an interval carries both effect size and
estimate precision, while a significance verdict carries neither yet is routinely read as
though it did. The one test retained is the Wilcoxon test above, where pairing is by
construction and the null of no saving is a question worth asking directly. Its $p$-value
is reported as a number against the single $\alpha$ of~\eqref{eq:method:bootstrap}, not as
a row of stars.
