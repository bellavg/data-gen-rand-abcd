\section{Data}
\label{sec:method:data}

The corpus turns a small set of circuits into many labelled training examples. Each source
design is expanded by randomized synthesis into thousands of intermediate optimization
states, and every stored graph carries the optimizability label $y(G)$ of~\eqref{eq:prelim:target}
under a fixed synthesis algorithm.

\subsection{Source Circuits}
\label{sec:method:data:sources}
The corpus is built from 55 source circuits, drawn from three collections and summarised
in~\ref{tab:source_circuits}. The characteristics of each design, measured on its
untransformed base AIG, are given in~\ref{tab:corpus_designs}.

Twenty-nine are the hardware IP designs of OpenABC-D \citep{chowdhury2021openabc}, which
collects them in turn from OpenCores, IWLS, the MIT Lincoln Laboratory CEP and OpenPiton.
They span bus and peripheral control (\texttt{spi}, \texttt{simple\_spi}, \texttt{i2c},
\texttt{sasc}, \texttt{ss\_pcm}, \texttt{usb\_phy}, \texttt{wb\_dma}, \texttt{wb\_conmax},
\texttt{ac97\_ctrl}, \texttt{pci}, \texttt{mem\_ctrl}, \texttt{ethernet},
\texttt{vga\_lcd}), cryptography (\texttt{aes}, \texttt{aes\_xcrypt},
\texttt{aes\_secworks}, \texttt{des3\_area}, \texttt{sha256}), signal processing
(\texttt{fir}, \texttt{iir}, \texttt{dft}, \texttt{idft}, \texttt{jpeg}), and processors and
SoC fragments (\texttt{tv80}, \texttt{tinyRocket}, \texttt{bp\_be}, \texttt{picosoc},
\texttt{fpu}, \texttt{dynamic\_node}).

All 47 non-synthetic designs entered the pipeline as BENCH netlists from the OpenABC-D
distribution, obtained from the NYU UltraViolet repository record
(\url{https://ultraviolet.library.nyu.edu/records/mw6q2-a8p15}) linked in the project's code
repository (\url{https://github.com/NYU-MLDA/OpenABC}). Its \texttt{bench} directory carries
the classical benchmarks of the following paragraph alongside the 29 IP designs the dataset
paper documents. Each netlist is converted to an AIG by a single ABC pass that reads it and
applies structural hashing, which is the only preprocessing applied before the
transformation of~\ref{sec:method:data:generation:transform}.

Eighteen more are classical combinational benchmarks that OpenABC-D does not include: the
eight arithmetic circuits of the EPFL suite \citep{amaru2015epfl} (\texttt{div},
\texttt{hyp}, \texttt{log2}, \texttt{max}, \texttt{multiplier}, \texttt{sin}, \texttt{sqrt},
\texttt{square}), four ISCAS-85 circuits \citep{brglez1985iscas} (\texttt{c1355},
\texttt{c5315}, \texttt{c6288}, \texttt{c7552}), and six MCNC/LGSynth circuits
\citep{yang1991mcnc} (\texttt{apex1}, \texttt{bc0}, \texttt{dalu}, \texttt{i10},
\texttt{k2}, \texttt{mainpla}). These extend the corpus into deep arithmetic, which
OpenABC-D deliberately excludes in favour of larger and functionally more diverse IP. The
effect on the depth distribution is pronounced. Median depth across the 47 designs outside
this suite is 31 levels and the deepest of them is \texttt{fpu} at 819, whereas the EPFL
circuits reach
4{,}373 (\texttt{div}), 5{,}059 (\texttt{sqrt}) and 24{,}802 (\texttt{hyp}). The three
deepest circuits in the corpus therefore all enter through this collection, and they set the
bound on the precomputed depth encoding
of~\ref{sec:method:data:representation:pe}.

The remaining eight are synthetic circuits, named \texttt{128} through \texttt{16384} after
their \emph{primary input count} rather than their size (\texttt{16384} has $16{,}384$ inputs
but $131{,}072$ AND gates). They are randomly sampled AIGs from the random AIG generator of
\texttt{mockturtle}, part of the EPFL logic synthesis libraries \citep{soeken2018epfl}. Each
new gate draws two operands uniformly at random from the signals built so far, the constant,
the primary inputs and every prior gate, and inverts each independently with probability
$1/2$. A draw that structurally hashes to an existing node or collapses to a trivial case is
discarded and redrawn, so every gate in the result is distinct. The gate budget is eight
times the input count throughout, doubling at each step from $128$ inputs / $1{,}024$ gates
to $16{,}384$ inputs / $131{,}072$ gates, and every node without a fanout at the end becomes a
primary output, between $37\%$ and $39\%$ of the gate count.

The generation script itself was not kept. This account is reconstructed from the eight
AIGs, which fix it exactly except for one parameter: the seed is not recoverable, so the
eight circuits cannot be regenerated independently
(\ref{sec:method:experiment:reproducibility}). The construction also explains their
behaviour under optimization, because fanins drawn uniformly at random with duplicates
rejected arrive already structurally hashed, with almost none of the repeated local
subfunctions that \texttt{rewrite}, \texttt{refactor} and \texttt{resub} exist to collapse.
Their near-zero optimizability (\ref{sec:method:data:label:distribution}) is therefore a
property of the sampling, not a measurement failure, sharpest in the largest circuits, where
the pool is widest and collisions rarest.

The license column of~\ref{tab:source_circuits} records each suite's own terms, but that
is not the term that governs the copies actually used, because the 18 classical benchmarks
were not obtained from their original distributions. Every non-synthetic netlist entered
the corpus through the OpenABC-D release, so those copies were received under its BSD
3-Clause license. The EPFL suite's MIT license applies in addition, and the ISCAS-85 and
MCNC circuits attach no formal terms of their own, leaving the BSD 3-Clause license of the
redistributing dataset as the only written term on the copies used. Any release of the
corpus is therefore governed by BSD 3-Clause attribution requirements, retaining the MIT
notice for the EPFL circuits (\ref{sec:discussion:ethics}).

The provenance is the point here, not the inventory, because the design-level split
(\ref{sec:method:experiment:splitting}) rests on the assumption that these 55 are genuinely
distinct circuits rather than variants of one another. Mostly they are: three different
sources, four benchmark traditions, and no design derived from another. The assumption is
not perfect, and the exceptions should be named rather than assumed away. The three AES
variants implement the same cipher; \texttt{dft} and \texttt{idft} are forward and inverse
transforms of one structure, as are \texttt{fir} and \texttt{iir} to a lesser degree; and
the eight synthetic circuits differ from each other only in scale. A split by design does
not guarantee that such a family lands entirely on one side of it, so a small amount of
family-level similarity can cross the train/test boundary even though no individual circuit
does. This is a weaker form of leakage than the one design-level splitting exists to close,
being a structural family resemblance rather than the same circuit in two optimization
states, but it bounds how strongly an unseen-design result can be read and is taken up
in~\ref{sec:discussion:limitations}.

Scale spans nearly three orders of magnitude, which is what forces the node-budget batching
of~\ref{sec:method:experiment:batching}. The base AIGs range from 403 AND nodes
(\texttt{ss\_pcm}) to 245{,}046 (\texttt{dft}) at a median of 11{,}464, and depth ranges
from 8 levels to 24{,}802 at a median of 35. Transformation and optimization move these
figures, so the bounds the preprocessing pipeline records over the full corpus are higher
still, at 366{,}040 AND nodes and a depth of 24{,}972.

\subsubsection{Structural Characteristics of the Source Designs}
\label{sec:method:data:sources:stats}
The remaining columns of~\ref{tab:corpus_designs} show the same spread. PI and PO counts
range from 24 (\texttt{sin}) to $37{,}603$ (\texttt{idft}) and from 11 (\texttt{bc0}) to
$49{,}307$ (\texttt{16384}), at medians of 494 and 398. Edge count tracks node count
directly, since every AND node contributes two edges and every output one, from 896
(\texttt{ss\_pcm}) to $527{,}509$ (\texttt{dft}) at a median of $25{,}065$. The inverted-edge
share that fixes the edge feature of~\ref{sec:method:data:representation:features} varies
from $33.8\%$ to $87.8\%$ of a design's edges, at a median of $57.2\%$.

\subsection{Dataset Generation}
\label{sec:method:data:generation}

\subsubsection{Random Structural Transformation}
\label{sec:method:data:generation:transform}
Each source design is expanded by applying random synthesis recipes to it and retaining
every intermediate result, following the construction of
OpenABC-D~\citep{chowdhury2021openabc}. A recipe is a sequence of $20$ transformations drawn
independently and uniformly from seven area-oriented passes: balancing, and rewriting,
refactoring and resubstitution each in a plain and a zero-cost variant, the latter accepting
replacements that leave node count unchanged. The same $200$ recipes, the first $200$ of the
$1{,}500$ released with OpenABC-D and all of them distinct, are applied to every design, so
recipe identity is not confounded with design identity.

The AIG is written after \emph{every} pass rather than only at the end, so a single recipe
contributes $20$ graphs, one per prefix of the recipe, plus one further graph after the
structural-choice pass that follows the transformation sequence: $21$ in total. Together with
the unmodified design itself this gives $1 + 200 \times 21 = 4{,}201$ base graphs per design,
and $55 \times 4{,}201 = 231{,}055$ overall. The recipes continue into a technology-mapping
tail, which does not execute because no standard-cell library is loaded in this flow, so no
graph beyond the structural-choice pass is produced. The per-design count is verified rather
than assumed, and generation is treated as failed unless exactly $4{,}201$ AIGs are present.
The pass alphabet, the recipe format and the unexecuted tail are listed
in~\ref{sec:apx:algorithms}.

Randomised transformation rather than plain replication is what makes this corpus a learning
problem at all. Replicating a design would yield identical graphs carrying identical labels,
adding weight but no information. A random recipe prefix instead yields a circuit that is
\emph{functionally} equivalent to its source, since every pass is equivalence-preserving, but
structurally different, and, critically, differently optimizable: how much a synthesis script
can still remove depends on which rewriting the graph has already absorbed. The corpus
therefore varies along exactly the axis the label measures while holding the function fixed.

That is also what makes the design-level split
(\ref{sec:method:experiment:splitting}) necessary rather than merely conservative. The
$4{,}201$ graphs derived from one design are not independent samples: they share a Boolean
function, a primary-input interface, and a large amount of common structure, and successive
prefixes of one recipe differ by a single pass. A random split over graphs would place
near-siblings of every test graph into training, and the resulting score would measure recall
of a design the model had already seen rather than generalization. Splitting on the design
partitions the corpus along the one boundary this construction never crosses.


The step-21 output of every recipe is not a plain AIG. It follows a structural-choice pass
that retains multiple structurally distinct but functionally equivalent implementations of a
signal side by side, a \emph{choice network}, rather than collapsing to one representative.
The synthesis tool's own node count describes only the chosen representative and is smaller
than $|\mathcal{V}(G)|$, the graph's true node count, whenever the pass finds such an
equivalence. Checked against the stored graphs, that undercount is confirmed in 43 of the
200 recipes at step 21. Step 21 is one of the 21 stored outputs per recipe, so it alone
accounts for 200 of the $4{,}201$ graphs generated per design, close to $4.8\%$ of the total.

The recorded optimizability for these graphs substitutes that smaller, tool-reported count
for $|\mathcal{V}(G)|$ in~\eqref{eq:prelim:target}, so $y(G)$ is computed against the wrong
denominator wherever the two disagree. The mismatch was found after training. The affected
labels were not recomputed, and the model was not retrained: the graphs remain in the
corpus and in every reported split, and no result in~\ref{sec:results} currently excludes
them. This is recorded here as a limitation of the reported accuracy rather than as a
resolved issue.

\subsubsection{Optimization}
\label{sec:method:data:generation:algorithms}
Four synthesis scripts were applied during dataset generation: \texttt{Orchestrate},
\texttt{Deepsyn}, \texttt{Syn4} and \texttt{C2RS}. They differ in how they search: some
apply a fixed sequence of rewriting, refactoring and balancing passes, while others explore
seeded or scored variations. Command templates and per-algorithm parameters are given
in~\ref{sec:apx:first_appendix}.

Only \texttt{Orchestrate} is used for training and evaluation in this thesis
(\ref{sec:intro:scope:algorithm}); the remaining three exist on disk and are the most
direct extension available (\ref{sec:conclusion:futurework:algorithms}).

\subsection{Tiered Dataset Structure}
\label{sec:method:data:tiers}
The corpus has two tiers of \emph{stored} graphs. Every stored graph is a training
example; the optimized results used to label them are not themselves retained, for the
reason given in~\ref{sec:method:data:tiers:labelling}.

\subsubsection{Tier 0: Base Graphs}
\label{sec:method:data:tier0}
This originates from 55 designs randomly transformed into 231,055 base graphs (Tier-0).

\subsubsection{Tier 1: Single-Algorithm Optimization}
\label{sec:method:data:tier1}
Applying the target synthesis algorithms to these base graphs yields exactly 231,055
primary (Tier-1) samples per algorithm. For the Orchestrate prediction task three of
them contribute samples (\texttt{Deepsyn}, \texttt{Syn4} and \texttt{C2RS}), while
\texttt{Orchestrate} itself does not: an Orchestrate-produced graph relabelled by
Orchestrate is precisely the sequential step
that~\ref{sec:method:data:tiers:labelling} stops at, so it is a label source rather than a
sample.

\subsubsection{Labelling and Tier Depth}
\label{sec:method:data:tiers:labelling}
Labelling a graph means running the target script on it and recording how many nodes it
removed (\ref{sec:method:data:label:definition}). The optimized \emph{output} of that run
is a graph too, so the construction could be iterated indefinitely; it is stopped after
one step, and the outputs of the labelling runs on tier-1 graphs are discarded rather than
promoted to a third tier.

Two consequences are worth stating, because both shape the results.

First, the two tiers are not independent of each other. Optimizing a tier-0 graph with
algorithm $S$ produces both that graph's label under $S$ \emph{and} the corresponding
tier-1 graph: one synthesis run serves both purposes. The tier-1 set is therefore exactly
the set of labelling outputs of tier 0.

Second, this is what bounds how optimizable the corpus is. A tier-1 graph has already been
through one full synthesis script, so what Orchestrate can still remove from it is small,
though how small depends far more sharply on \emph{which} script ran first than on the tier
itself (\ref{fig:dataset_label_by_source}), to the point that the tier ordering inverts and
tier 1 carries the higher mean target. That decomposition is taken up
in~\ref{sec:method:data:statistics:source}. Roughly half the evaluation corpus has an
optimizability of exactly zero (\ref{fig:dataset_zero_inflation}). That is a property of
the construction rather than a defect, but every $R^2$ in~\ref{sec:results} has to be read
against it (\ref{sec:method:data:label:distribution}).

\subsection{Target Label: Optimizability}
\label{sec:method:data:label}

\subsubsection{Definition}
\label{sec:method:data:label:definition}
For an AIG $G$ and synthesis algorithm $S$, the optimizability target is the relative node
reduction $y(G)$ of~\eqref{eq:prelim:target}, formulated as a regression task, and is not
restated here. The bound motivates the terminal sigmoid in the prediction
head (\ref{sec:method:architecture}): the model cannot predict outside the achievable range.

Note what this does and does not measure. It is a \emph{relative} quantity, so it is
comparable across circuits of different sizes, which is what makes a single model over a
heterogeneous corpus meaningful, but it is also therefore harder to predict than an
absolute count, since the model must infer both what will be removed and what the graph
started with. It is an area proxy and says nothing about delay or power
(\ref{sec:intro:scope:target}).

\subsubsection{Label Distribution}
\label{sec:method:data:label:distribution}
The target is heavily concentrated near zero (mean $0.020$, standard deviation $0.053$,
and just under half of all graphs at exactly zero), and what determines it is the synthesis
script a graph came from rather than the circuit or the tier. Both facts constrain how the
results can be read, and both are set out with the measurements
in~\ref{sec:method:data:statistics:label} and~\ref{sec:method:data:statistics:source} rather
than repeated here.

\subsection{Graph Representation}
\label{sec:method:data:representation}

\subsubsection{Node and Edge Features}
\label{sec:method:data:representation:features}
Each AIG is represented as an attributed graph carrying one-hot node types (constant,
primary input, AND, primary output), a one-hot edge inversion flag (regular connection or
inverted signal), and a topological depth encoding. The AIGER files are read and traversed with
\textsc{aigverse} \citep{walter2025aigverse}, an interface to the same EPFL logic synthesis
libraries \citep{soeken2018epfl}.

\subsubsection{Positional Encoding Precomputation}
\label{sec:method:data:representation:pe}
Topological levels are computed once, offline, and stored on the graph; the model reads the
stored attribute rather than recomputing it. This matters for more than speed. Levels are
computed on the \emph{unreduced} graph and then carried through reduction, so a partitioned
or sparsified graph retains the depth information of the circuit it came from even where the
edges that establish that depth have been cut. A runtime encoding would instead describe the
reduced graph's own topology, which is a different and, for this task, less informative
quantity.

\subsection{Dataset Analysis}
\label{sec:method:data:statistics}
\providecommand{\resultsfigs}{media/results/figures}
\providecommand{\resultsgen}{media/results/tables}
This subsection characterises the corpus the model is actually trained and evaluated on: its
scale, its structure, the shape of the target, and how the two evaluation splits differ from
one another. Several decisions made elsewhere in this chapter rest on the distributions
reported here rather than on argument: the graph-scale bound
of~\ref{sec:method:experiment:scale}, the node-budget batching
of~\ref{sec:method:experiment:batching}, the terminal sigmoid
of~\ref{sec:method:architecture}. Every $R^2$ in~\ref{sec:results} has to be read
against them.

One scope caveat applies throughout, and it is not a small one. Except for
\ref{tab:corpus_stats}, which covers every stored graph, what follows is measured on the
\emph{evaluation splits}, 96{,}430 test graphs over six designs and 80{,}525
validation graphs over five, rather than over the full corpus
of~\ref{sec:method:data:tiers}. The splits are design-disjoint samples of the same
construction, so the structural statistics can be read as representative; the label
statistics cannot, because which designs land in a split changes them substantially
(\ref{sec:method:data:statistics:splits}).

\subsubsection{Scale and Structure}
\label{sec:method:data:statistics:scale}
Graph size spans nearly three orders of magnitude within the evaluation splits alone: a
median of 15{,}977 nodes against a maximum of 232{,}715
(\ref{fig:dataset_size_distribution}, \ref{tab:dataset_stats}). This is the distribution
that forces node-budget batching rather than a fixed graph count per batch
(\ref{sec:method:experiment:batching}); at a fixed batch size the largest graphs in the
corpus are four orders of magnitude more expensive than the smallest.

Structurally the graphs are far more uniform than they are in size
(\ref{fig:dataset_structure}, \ref{tab:dataset_structure}). AND gates and the constant
account for $82.1\%$ of nodes on average and the primary-input/output interface for the
remaining $17.9\%$, and the graphs are sparse and near-tree-like at $1.75$ edges per node.
The AND-gate share is not a descriptive statistic here but a mechanism: the
\texttt{and\_gate\_only} sparsification of~\ref{sec:method:sparse:andgate} drops exactly the
interface nodes, so $82.1\%$ \emph{is} its node retention, and the spread on that share
($63.3\%$ to $99.9\%$ across graphs) is why its compression varies between circuits rather
than being a dial that can be set.

\subsubsection{Measured Target Distribution}
\label{sec:method:data:statistics:label}
The target is extremely concentrated near zero
(\ref{fig:dataset_label_distribution}, \ref{tab:dataset_stats}). Pooled over the test split
the mean optimizability is $0.020$ with a standard deviation of $0.053$, and $73.4\%$ of
graphs sit below $y = 0.005$. Just under half, $48.8\%$, are \emph{exactly} zero: Orchestrate
removes nothing at all.

Two consequences follow, and both shape how the results chapter can be read. First, $R^2$ is
a ratio against the target's own variance, and that variance is small, so a respectable RMSE
is entirely compatible with a near-zero or negative $R^2$; the baseline diagnosis
of~\ref{sec:method:architecture:baselines} turns on precisely this. Second, with a point mass
of this size at zero, a constant predictor is a strong baseline, and any model has to beat it
on the minority of graphs where something is actually removable.

Where that point mass sits is not uniform (\ref{fig:dataset_zero_inflation}). It concentrates
in the smallest circuits, which are already minimal, and in the synthetic designs: the design
\texttt{16384} contributes $16{,}080$ test graphs, a sixth of the split, with a mean
target of $0.0000$, a maximum of $0.0003$ and $92.4\%$ of graphs at exactly zero. Orchestrate
is at a fixed point on it, for the reasons given
in~\ref{sec:method:data:sources}. The joint distribution of size and label
(\ref{fig:dataset_size_vs_label}) is the entire signal available to the size-only baseline
of~\ref{sec:results:rq1:baselines}, and it shows why that baseline is not trivial: graph size
is largely a proxy for ``is this optimizable at all''.

\subsubsection{Determinants of the Target}
\label{sec:method:data:statistics:source}
The strongest single determinant of the target is not the circuit but the synthesis script
that produced the graph (\ref{fig:dataset_label_by_source}). A tier-1 graph has already been
optimized once, and how much Orchestrate can still remove depends overwhelmingly on which
script ran first. Pooled over both evaluation splits, Syn4-derived graphs have a mean
optimizability of $0.046$ with only $11.7\%$ at exactly zero, while Deepsyn- and
C2RS-derived graphs average $0.0022$ and $0.0011$ with $54.4\%$ and $52.1\%$ at zero. That is
a twenty- to forty-fold difference in the target, driven by a property of the label pipeline
rather than of the circuit.

This is also what explains an apparent anomaly in~\ref{tab:dataset_stats}: tier-1 graphs have
a \emph{higher} mean target ($0.023$) than tier-0 graphs ($0.010$), although
\ref{sec:method:data:tiers:labelling} argues that a graph which has already been through a
full synthesis script should have less left to remove. The elevation is entirely attributable
to one of tier 1's three sources. On the test split the three source means are $0.0015$
(C2RS), $0.0025$ (Deepsyn) and $0.0654$ (Syn4), whose average is $0.023$, exactly the
tier-1 figure. Excluding the Syn4 third, tier 1 averages $0.0020$, five times \emph{below}
tier 0. The tier is therefore the weaker cut of the data, and the source script should be
reported alongside it wherever tier-wise results appear.

\subsubsection{Composition of the Evaluation Splits}
\label{sec:method:data:statistics:splits}
Under a design-level split the test set is a handful of whole circuits rather than a random
sample (\ref{fig:dataset_design_composition}), so every pooled test metric is an average over
six designs and their particular mix. With one of those six contributing a sixth of the
graphs at a constant-zero target, the pooled figures are sensitive to composition in a way
that a random split would have hidden.

Validation and test are correspondingly not exchangeable
(\ref{fig:dataset_split_comparison}, \ref{tab:dataset_composition}). They are disjoint
circuit sets, and they differ on every axis that matters: $26.2\%$ of validation graphs sit
at $y = 0$ against $48.8\%$ of test, the test maximum target is more than twice the
validation maximum ($0.274$ against $0.114$), and the validation graphs are larger at the
median ($29{,}399$ nodes against $15{,}977$). The divergence is sharpest for the Syn4 subset,
where not one validation graph has a target of exactly zero against $21.4\%$ of the
corresponding test graphs. This is expected under a design-level split rather than a defect,
but it is the direct explanation for the validation-to-test gap
in~\ref{fig:rq3_val_test_gap}, and it bounds how far validation-based model selection can be
assumed to transfer.
