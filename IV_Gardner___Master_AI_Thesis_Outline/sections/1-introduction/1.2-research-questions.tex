\section{Research Questions}
\label{sec:intro:rqs}

\subsection{RQ1: Task Feasibility and Baseline}
\label{sec:intro:rq1}
How well can a GNN predict the optimizability of a circuit directly from
its full, unreduced AIG representation, relative to trivial predictors and published
circuit-representation models?

\subsubsection{RQ1a: Protocol Sensitivity}
How much of the RQ1 result is attributable to the assignment of graphs to splits rather than
to the encoder? One model is trained under three protocols differing in nothing but that
assignment: a random split, a recipe-disjoint split withholding whole transformation recipes,
and the design-disjoint split withholding whole source circuits, the protocol behind every
result reported here (\ref{sec:method:experiment:splitting}). The three are nested in what each
admits into the test set, and the prediction under test is an ordering of the $R^{2}$
of~\eqref{eq:method:r2},
\begin{equation}
    R^{2}_{\mathrm{random}} \;\geq\; R^{2}_{\mathrm{recipe}} \;\geq\; R^{2}_{\mathrm{design}},
    \label{eq:intro:protocol-ordering}
\end{equation}
whose two gaps measure design recognition rather than structural inference
(\ref{sec:results:rq1:protocol}).

\subsection{RQ2: Reduction Efficiency}
\label{sec:intro:rq2}
Applied to AIGs, how do partitioning, sparsification and summarization/coarsening differ in
retention $\eta_{\mathcal{V}}, \eta_{\mathcal{E}}$ of~\eqref{eq:prelim:retention}, peak memory,
training and inference throughput, and the offline cost of the reduction itself?

\subsection{RQ3: Predictive Retention and Trade-offs}
\label{sec:intro:rq3}
To what extent do models trained on reduced AIGs retain predictive accuracy (Smooth L1 loss,
RMSE, $R^2$) and circuit-ranking ability (Spearman correlation) relative to the full-graph
baseline of RQ1, and which methods retain the most accuracy per unit of memory and time saved?

\subsubsection{RQ3a: Exact and Inexact Compression}
\label{sec:intro:rq3a}
How much compression do AIGs admit at zero accuracy cost, and what does each further
increment cost once that bound is passed? The provably lossless coarsening
of~\ref{sec:method:architecture:exact} fixes that bound at its retention ratios
$\eta_{\mathcal{V}}$ and $\eta_{\mathcal{E}}$, so every inexact method is quantified by the
compression it gains beyond that bound and the accuracy it forfeits.

\subsection{RQ4: Value of Domain-Informed Adaptation}
\label{sec:intro:rq4}
Do domain-informed reductions, which exploit topological level, reconvergence structure or
gate role, retain more predictive accuracy than their generic counterparts at matched
retention?

\subsection{RQ5: Cross-State Inference}
\label{sec:intro:rq5}
Can a model trained exclusively on reduced AIGs, whether partitioned, sparsified or
summarized, be queried on full ones at inference time without losing predictive accuracy?

A \emph{structural state} is the form in which a graph reaches the encoder, the reduced
$R(G)$ or the unreduced $G$ of~\ref{sec:prelim:formalization}, and cross-state inference
evaluates weights trained in one state on inputs in the other.

The training and inference costs of~\ref{sec:intro:motivation:scale} differ by a factor of the
encoder depth $L$. Training on $R(G)$ and querying on $G$ therefore stay inside one memory
budget when
\begin{equation}
    \left| \mathcal{V}(G) \right| + \left| \mathcal{E}(G) \right|
    \;\leq\; L \left( \left| \mathcal{V}(R(G)) \right|
        + \left| \mathcal{E}(R(G)) \right| \right),
    \label{eq:intro:crossstate-budget}
\end{equation}
so any reduction retaining more than a fraction $1/L$ of $G$ admits full-graph inference on
the hardware its own training required.

\subsection{Scope and Delimitations}
\label{sec:intro:rqs:scope}
Optimizability prediction is the evaluation task, not the object of study. The delimitations
below were fixed in advance. Limitations found during the work are reported
in~\ref{sec:discussion:limitations}.

\paragraph{Target synthesis algorithm.}
\label{sec:intro:scope:algorithm}
Training and evaluation target one synthesis algorithm, since variance across algorithms would
confound the cost attributable to a reduction. The corpus carries three further algorithms, left unused
(\ref{sec:method:data:tier1}, \ref{sec:conclusion:futurework:algorithms}).

\paragraph{Graph scale.}
\label{sec:intro:scope:scale}
RQ1's full-graph baseline must fit in memory, so the corpus is bounded at an intermediate
rather than an industrial scale (\ref{sec:method:experiment:scale},
\ref{sec:discussion:limitations:scalability}).

\paragraph{Definition of optimizability.}
\label{sec:intro:scope:target}
Optimizability is relative \emph{node} reduction only. Area, delay and power, the objectives
synthesis actually optimizes for, are not modelled
(\ref{sec:discussion:limitations:validity}).

\paragraph{Reduction families.}
\label{sec:intro:scope:reductions}
Partitioning, sparsification and summarization/coarsening are covered, the taxonomy of
\citet{hashemi2024survey} with partitioning substituted for condensation, which establishes
no node correspondence and leaves RQ5 undefined (\ref{sec:relwork:reduction:summarization}).

\paragraph{Model architecture.}
\label{sec:intro:scope:architecture}
One encoder, an edge-aware GCN+ (\ref{sec:method:architecture}), held fixed so that outcome
differences are attributable to the reduction, which leaves transfer to another encoder
untested.

\paragraph{Prediction rather than script generation.}
\label{sec:intro:scope:prediction}
Optimizability is predicted, not synthesis scripts: selecting a script would require ranking
\emph{algorithms} for one circuit, which a single-algorithm model cannot support
(\ref{sec:conclusion:futurework:generation}).

\subsection{Problem Formalization}
\label{sec:prelim:formalization}
Let $G$ be an AIG with the node roles
of~\eqref{eq:prelim:role} and edge inversions of~\eqref{eq:prelim:inversion} as its features,
and let $S$ be a deterministic synthesis algorithm
(\ref{sec:prelim:algorithms:orchestrate}) mapping $G$ to the function-equivalent,
optimized $S(G)$. The \emph{optimizability} of $G$ under $S$ is the fraction of nodes the
algorithm removes,
\begin{equation}
    y(G) = 1 - \frac{\left| \mathcal{V}(S(G)) \right|}{\left| \mathcal{V}(G) \right|}
    \;\in\; [0, 1].
    \label{eq:prelim:target}
\end{equation}
Evaluating~\eqref{eq:prelim:target} requires executing $S$, the cost prediction avoids. An
encoder $f_\theta$, a GNN in the sense of~\ref{sec:prelim:gnn:mp} with a graph-level readout,
is fitted to approximate $y$ and takes values in the same interval $[0, 1]$. A reduction
operator $R$ (\ref{sec:prelim:reduction}) acts ahead of the encoder, so the argument $f_\theta$
receives is $G$ or $R(G)$.

\emph{Compression} is reported separately as the node and edge retention ratios
$\eta_{\mathcal{V}}$ and $\eta_{\mathcal{E}}$ of~\eqref{eq:prelim:retention}. The two are not
interchangeable: several methods hold $\eta_{\mathcal{V}} = 1$ while lowering
$\eta_{\mathcal{E}}$ alone. Memory and time differences are taken per graph against
the full-graph baseline and carry a $95\%$ bootstrap interval and a two-sided paired rank test,
whereas accuracy differences are single-run point estimates
(\ref{sec:method:experiment:metrics:stats}).
