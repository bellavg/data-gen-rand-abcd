\section{Research Questions}
\label{sec:intro:rqs}

\subsection{Problem Formalization}
\label{sec:intro:formalization}
An AIG $G = (\mathcal{V}, \mathcal{E})$ holds one node per primary input, one per primary
output, a single constant node, and a set of AND gates $\mathcal{V}_{\wedge}(G) \subseteq
\mathcal{V}$, each with exactly two incoming edges. Every edge records whether the signal it
carries is inverted (\ref{sec:prelim:aig}). The \emph{target script} $S$, whose effect on
$G$ is the quantity predicted, maps $G$ to a function-equivalent circuit $S(G)$ with the
same primary inputs, primary outputs, and constant. $S$ only keeps a rewrite that does not
increase the AND gate count, so AND gates are the only thing it ever removes, never adds.
The \emph{optimizability} of $G$ under $S$ is the fraction of them it does remove,
\begin{equation}
    y_S(G) = 1 - \frac{\left| \mathcal{V}_{\wedge}(S(G)) \right|}{\left| \mathcal{V}_{\wedge}(G) \right|}
    \;\in\; [0, 1],
    \label{eq:intro:target}
\end{equation}
so $y_S(G) = 0$ when $S$ removes no AND gates. Measuring $y_S(G)$ means running $S$.
Predicting it does not.

A GNN with a graph-level readout (\ref{sec:prelim:gnn:mp}), denoted $f_\theta$, is fitted to
approximate $y_S$ for the single script $S$ this thesis targets
(\ref{sec:intro:scope:algorithm}). A \emph{reduction} $R$ maps $G$ to a smaller training
input $R(G)$, one graph or, under partitioning, a set of $k$ subgraphs, before the model sees it
(\ref{sec:prelim:reduction}). The \emph{node retention} $\eta_{\mathcal{V}}$ and \emph{edge
retention} $\eta_{\mathcal{E}}$ of a reduction are the fractions of nodes and of edges it
retains,
\begin{equation}
    \eta_{\mathcal{V}} = \frac{\left| \mathcal{V}(R(G)) \right|}{\left| \mathcal{V}(G) \right|},
    \qquad
    \eta_{\mathcal{E}} = \frac{\left| \mathcal{E}(R(G)) \right|}{\left| \mathcal{E}(G) \right|},
    \label{eq:intro:retention}
\end{equation}
where either ratio equals $1$ when nothing was removed. The two are not interchangeable:
sparsification lowers $\eta_{\mathcal{E}}$ at $\eta_{\mathcal{V}} = 1$. Partitioning is the
opposite case: its $k$ subgraphs are node-disjoint, so $\eta_{\mathcal{V}} = 1$ regardless of
$k$, and only the edges it cuts between subgraphs are dropped, so $\eta_{\mathcal{E}}$
together with $k$ reports what partitioning costs.
Accuracy is reported as predictive error (Smooth L1 loss, RMSE, and the coefficient of
determination $R^2$) and as ranking ability, the Spearman correlation between predicted and
true optimizability across the test circuits. Cost is reported as peak memory and time per graph
(\ref{sec:method:experiment:metrics}).

\subsection{RQ1: Task Feasibility and Baseline}
\label{sec:intro:rq1}
Can $f_\theta$ predict $y_S(G)$~\eqref{eq:intro:target} from the unreduced AIG $G$ alone, on
designs withheld from training? Accuracy is measured against two references: the training-set
mean $\bar{y}_S$ and existing GNN models for circuits, retrained on the same data to predict
$y_S$ and evaluated on the same withheld graphs (\ref{sec:method:architecture:baselines}).

\paragraph{Sensitivity to the evaluation protocol.}
The corpus supplies many graphs per design, so a train/test split can be drawn in three ways.
The question above withholds all graphs from the test designs (design-disjoint). The
alternatives withhold individual graphs (random) or whole \emph{generating scripts}, the
sequences that produced the corpus graphs from their designs (script-disjoint). Under both,
every design stays in training. How much do the RQ1 results change under each
(\ref{sec:method:experiment:splitting},~\ref{sec:results:rq1:protocol})?

\subsection{RQ2: Cost of Reduction}
\label{sec:intro:rq2}
This work studies three classes of graph reduction (\ref{sec:intro:scope:reductions}):
partitioning cuts a graph into $k$ subgraphs that are trained on separately, sparsification
deletes edges and keeps every node, and summarization/coarsening collapses groups of nodes
into super-nodes. Applied to an AIG $G$, how do the methods in these three classes differ in
node retention $\eta_{\mathcal{V}}$ and edge retention $\eta_{\mathcal{E}}$
\eqref{eq:intro:retention}, peak memory, training and inference throughput, and the one-time
cost of computing $R(G)$? Accuracy is left to RQ3.

\subsection{RQ3: Predictive Retention and Trade-offs}
\label{sec:intro:rq3}
\emph{Predictive retention} is the accuracy a model trained on $R(G)$ keeps relative to one
trained on the unreduced AIG $G$ (\ref{sec:intro:rq1}), measured on the same withheld
designs. How much predictive retention does each reduction method reach at the node retention
$\eta_{\mathcal{V}}$ and edge retention $\eta_{\mathcal{E}}$ it achieves, and how much memory
and time does it save per unit of accuracy lost (\ref{sec:method:experiment:metrics})?

\paragraph{The exact reference point.}
\citet{bollen2023exact} give a coarsening that, under stated conditions on the model, leaves
the prediction provably unchanged, i.e.\ $f_\theta$ returns the same value on the reduced
graph $R(G)$ as on $G$. To what extent do those conditions hold for an AIG $G$, specifically
with and without node-level information such as the topological level $\ell(v)$? What node
retention $\eta_{\mathcal{V}}$ and edge retention $\eta_{\mathcal{E}}$ does the coarsening
reach in each case? To what extent do inexact coarsenings, which do not carry the guarantee,
differ from it in node and edge retention and in predictive retention?

\subsection{RQ4: Value of AIG-Specific Adaptation}
\label{sec:intro:rq4}
A \emph{domain-agnostic} reduction decides what to remove or collapse from connectivity and
from node and edge attributes, without reading what those attributes mean in a circuit. An
\emph{AIG-specific} reduction is the same method under a criterion that does read them: a
node's topological level $\ell(v)$, its fanout, or its role as a gate. To what extent do
AIG-specific reductions reach higher predictive retention than their domain-agnostic
counterparts at comparable node retention $\eta_{\mathcal{V}}$ and edge retention
$\eta_{\mathcal{E}}$? For partitioning, where $\eta_{\mathcal{V}} = 1$ regardless of scheme
(\ref{sec:intro:formalization}), the pairing instead holds the partition count $k$ and edge
retention $\eta_{\mathcal{E}}$ comparable. Either pairing fixes the amount removed, so any
difference in predictive retention is attributable to which nodes and edges each criterion
selected (\ref{sec:results:rq4:pairings}).

\subsection{RQ5: Cross-State Inference}
\label{sec:intro:rq5}
A graph reaches the model in one of two \emph{structural states}: reduced, as $R(G)$, or
unreduced, as $G$. A model trained exclusively on reduced AIGs, whether partitioned,
sparsified, or summarized, is queried on the unreduced AIG $G$ of each withheld design, with
the same weights and no further training. To what extent does its accuracy there match the
accuracy those same weights reach on $R(G)$ for the same designs
(\ref{sec:results:rq5:accuracy})?

\subsection{Scope and Delimitations}
\label{sec:intro:rqs:scope}
The object of study is graph reduction as a preprocessing step for GNN training on AIGs.
Optimizability prediction is the task it is evaluated on. The delimitations below were fixed
in advance. Limitations found during the work are reported
in~\ref{sec:discussion:limitations}.

\paragraph{Target script.}
\label{sec:intro:scope:algorithm}
Training and evaluation target one script, \texttt{orchestrate}
(\ref{sec:prelim:algorithms:orchestrate}), since variance across scripts would confound the
cost attributable to a reduction.

\paragraph{Graph scale.}
\label{sec:intro:scope:scale}
The RQ1 baseline trains on the unreduced $G$, which must fit in memory, so the corpus is
bounded at $400{,}000$ nodes per AIG, below the millions of nodes an industrial design
reaches (\ref{sec:method:experiment:scale},~\ref{sec:discussion:limitations:scalability}).

\paragraph{Definition of optimizability.}
\label{sec:intro:scope:target}
Optimizability $y_S(G)$~\eqref{eq:intro:target} is defined on AND gate counts, the standard
area proxy on an AIG (\ref{sec:intro:motivation:synthesis}). Delay and power are not modeled
(\ref{sec:discussion:limitations:validity}).

\paragraph{Reduction classes.}
\label{sec:intro:scope:reductions}
Three classes of reduction are in scope: partitioning, sparsification, and
summarization/coarsening. This set is the taxonomy of \citet{hashemi2024survey} with
partitioning in place of condensation. Condensation synthesizes a new small graph whose nodes
correspond to no nodes of the original, so one set of weights cannot be applied to both
reduced and unreduced inputs, which RQ5 requires
(\ref{sec:relwork:reduction:summarization}). Condensation is left to future work.

\paragraph{Model architecture.}
\label{sec:intro:scope:architecture}
One architecture is held fixed, an edge-aware extension of GCN+ \citep{luo2024gnnplus} whose
messages read whether an edge is inverted as well as the nodes it joins
(\ref{sec:method:architecture}), so that outcome differences are attributable to the
reduction.

\paragraph{What the prediction supports.}
\label{sec:intro:scope:prediction}
The model predicts $y_S(G)$ for the one target script $S$, which says what fraction of a
circuit's AND gates $S$ would remove, and therefore whether running it is worthwhile. It
cannot choose a script. Choosing one requires comparing candidate scripts on the same
circuit, which a model fitted to a single $S$ has no way to do
(\ref{sec:conclusion:futurework:generation}).
