\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:intro:contributions}
This thesis studies graph reduction on whole AIGs. It measures what reduction costs as a
preprocessing step for GNN training, asks whether AIG-specific reductions reach higher
predictive retention than domain-agnostic ones at comparable node and edge retention, and
reports its primary results under a protocol that withholds whole designs.
Six contributions follow. (i) A corpus of AIGs extending OpenABC-D
\citep{chowdhury2021openabc} (\ref{sec:method:data}). (ii) A baseline predicting $y_S(G)$
from the unreduced AIG $G$ alone on withheld designs, and a systematic benchmark of
partitioning, sparsification, and summarization/coarsening against it on node and edge retention
($\eta_{\mathcal{V}}$, $\eta_{\mathcal{E}}$), peak memory, training and inference throughput,
the one-time cost of $R(G)$, and predictive retention. The baseline is measured against the
training-set mean $\bar{y}_S$ and against existing GNN models for circuits, retrained on the
same data (\ref{sec:method:architecture:baselines}).
(iii) AIG-specific adaptations of each class, built from a node's topological level
$\ell(v)$, its fanout, or its role as a gate, compared against their domain-agnostic
counterparts at comparable $\eta_{\mathcal{V}}$ and $\eta_{\mathcal{E}}$
(\ref{sec:results:rq4:pairings}). (iv) A coarsening for an AIG $G$ derived from
\citet{bollen2023exact} and the conditions under which it is exact and performant for
$f_\theta$ (\ref{sec:method:architecture:exact},~\ref{sec:method:summary:level}).
(v) An evaluation of cross-state inference, in which a model
trained only on reduced AIGs is queried, with the same weights, on the unreduced $G$ of each
withheld design (\ref{sec:results:rq5:accuracy}). (vi) A controlled comparison of three
train/test protocols: random, script-disjoint, and design-disjoint, ordered by the
information each admits into the test set
(\ref{sec:method:experiment:splitting},~\ref{sec:results:rq1:protocol}). The hypothesis under
test is that the design-disjoint split alone measures structural inference, and that the
excess accuracy of the other two is script and design recognition.

The corpus is the enabling contribution: it draws on 55 designs, the 29 of OpenABC-D plus 26
more. Every design withheld for testing is one the model cannot train on, so the number of
designs sets how large a design-disjoint test split can be
(\ref{sec:relwork:ml4eda:protocols}).
