\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:intro:motivation}
\subsection{Logic Synthesis and Optimization Script Design}
\label{sec:intro:motivation:synthesis}
Electronic Design Automation (EDA) supplies the tools that convert formal specifications
into the integrated circuits underlying nearly every computing device modern life depends on,
from consumer electronics to the data centers and communication networks behind them. Logic
synthesis and optimization remain a foundational steps within EDA for translating
high-level Boolean specifications into efficient, manufacturable hardware. One of the
central challenges of this field is efficiently representing and manipulating Boolean functions,
particularly as the complexity of digital circuits increases. Traditional methods such as
truth tables, sum-of-products (SOP), or Binary Decision Diagrams (BDD) became impractical for
large-scale designs due to their (potential) exponential growth in size. And-Inverter Graphs
(AIGs) address this issue by providing a more scalable and compact data structure for Boolean
function representation \citep{kuehlmann2002robust}, and are the state-of-the-art for
technology-independent logic optimization, supporting a wide range of synthesis and
optimization tasks fundamental to EDA \citep{mishchenko2006rewriting,brayton2010abc}. Over the
past few decades, numerous optimization algorithms have been developed for AIGs, each
targeting a different objective, among them node count, logic depth, area, delay, and power
consumption \citep{mishchenko2006scalable}.
%TODO citations

These algorithms are applied as a sequence, and their order is itself a determinant of the
outcome: the same set of algorithms yields different results depending on the order in which
they are applied. Crafting an effective sequence, a synthesis \emph{script}, for a given
circuit is therefore a heuristic trial-and-error process: each candidate script must run to
completion before its result is known.

\subsection{Machine Learning for AIGs}
\label{sec:intro:motivation:ml}

\subsubsection{Machine Learning for Logic Synthesis}
\label{sec:intro:motivation:ml:field}
Structural representations such as AIGs  provide a foundation for applying machine learning
(ML) to address a multitude of complex challenges in logic synthesis. These methods divide
into two classes: those that predict a synthesis outcome, and those that replace the
heuristic that produces it \citep{liu2026ml4lssurvey}. Progress on both fronts remains
constrained by the absence of standardized, large-scale AIG datasets and benchmarks
\citep{chowdhury2023imagenets}.

\subsubsection{Predicting Optimizability}
\label{sec:intro:motivation:ml:optimizability}
A model that predicts a circuit's \emph{optimizability}, the fraction of nodes a given
script removes from it, would circumvent that trial-and-error search by supporting
data-driven script generation directly, and, arguably, by revealing how much a circuit can
shrink before an expensive optimization flow completes. \emph{Graph Neural Networks} (GNNs) are the natural
class of models for the task: an AIG is a graph, and the quantity to be predicted is
structural. Industrial AIGs reach millions of nodes, at which point training a GNN on them
exceeds the memory available on a single device and drives training time beyond what is
practical, actively preventing the field from scaling to more capable models and more complex tasks.

\subsection{The Scale Bottleneck}
\label{sec:intro:motivation:scale}
To bypass this bottleneck, the usual responses are to restrict model capacity, adopting a
weaker architecture than the task warrants, or to rely on multi-GPU clusters and distributed
training. Neither removes the constraint: a single AIG can still exceed the memory of one
device, and a distributed setup only makes the problem explicit, since it necessitates
splitting the graph.

This ceiling applies to training, not inference. Backpropagation retains the node states,
materialized edge messages, and gradients of every layer, whereas a forward-only pass keeps
only one layer resident and computes no gradients. Training
therefore requires several times the memory inference does, scaling with the number of
layers. A circuit too large to train on may consequently still be queried, motivating the question of whether reducing the training
input alone suffices.

\subsection{Limitations of Generic Graph Reduction}
\label{sec:intro:motivation:reduction}
Graph reduction, applied as a preprocessing step before training, is standard practice on
citation and social graphs, but AIGs differ structurally: an edge encodes a term of
the Boolean function the circuit computes, and logic depth, the length of the longest path
from primary inputs to primary outputs, correlates with the circuit delay a synthesis pass
optimizes for. A cut that balances partition sizes, or a sparsification that preserves the
graph's spectrum, can blindly sever causal cones, obscure logic depth, and break the longest
paths, thereby altering the underlying logic function and destroying the structural
features accurate prediction depends on.

Merging rather than cutting fails differently. A coarsening that collapses gates into one
super-node loses the level separation between them unless its merge criterion reads or understands level, and
a criterion that merges gates by logical equivalence carries out part of the optimization a
synthesis pass would have carried out, shrinking the very quantity a predictor is asked to
estimate.

\subsection{Problem Statement and Research Gap}
\label{sec:intro:gap}
The field scales GNNs on AIGs in two ways today. The first is to train on extracted
subcircuits of at most a few thousand gates, a crude sampling that discards global structure
and is the de-facto reduction in most circuit-GNN work, including the DeepGate line
\citep{li2022deepgate}. The second is to scale the \emph{architecture}: DeepGate3
\citep{shi2024deepgate3} pools over subcircuits with a transformer, and DeepGate4
\citep{deepgate4_2025} uses sparse attention for sub-linear memory. Both change the model,
not the input.

Graph reduction shrinks the \emph{input} instead, and is well studied on citation, social
and knowledge graphs \citep{hashemi2024survey}. It has never been systematically applied to
whole AIGs as a preprocessing step for GNN training, and no work measures which reduction
preserves a downstream synthesis label.

The gap this thesis addresses is therefore twofold: (i) there is no systematic evaluation
of graph reduction for AIGs, and (ii) there is no evidence on whether AIG-specific
adaptation of those reductions is worth its cost.
